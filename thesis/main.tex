\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\singlespacing
This thesis investigates short-horizon price forecasting in Kalshi sports
prediction markets across Major League Baseball, the National Basketball
Association and the National Football League. A neural model combines
recurrent play-by-play representations with message passing among winner,
spread and total contracts to forecast price changes over 30--120 seconds.
Evaluation separates forecast accuracy from trading performance through
chronological development folds, a later out-of-sample period and live paper trading with
recorded order-book replay. Spread and total forecasts improve on a no-change
benchmark, while winner-contract skill is limited. Ablations
identify structural price inputs and cross-contract message passing as useful
sources of predictive information. Simulated development returns are positive across the six traded
market groups, but later out-of-sample performance is less uniform. The forward
evaluation records positive net returns in four of six groups, with losses on
MLB and NBA totals. Live observations show how stale reference prices, delayed
game feeds and adverse selection can limit executable returns. The
study supports relational price forecasting while showing that
historical forecast skill and simulated returns do not establish reliable
live profitability.

\chapter*{Key Terminology}\label{sec:terms}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Key Terminology}
The following Kalshi and trading terms are provided for readers without a
financial background.

\begin{description}
  \item[Contract and price.] A contract pays \$1 if its event happens, else \$0.
        Prices are quoted in cents. A price of 63 cents is commonly interpreted
        as an implied probability of approximately 63\%, subject to trading
        costs and other qualifications discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}.
  \item[Winner, spread and total.] A \emph{winner} contract concerns which
        team wins. A \emph{spread} contract concerns the winning margin,
        such as whether a team wins by more than 3.5 points. A \emph{total}
        contract concerns the combined score, such as whether it exceeds
        8.5 runs. A game can have many spread and total thresholds.
  \item[Order book, bid and ask.] The \emph{bid} is the highest price a buyer is
        currently willing to pay; the \emph{ask} is the lowest price a seller
        will accept. The gap between them is the \emph{spread} of the book
        (not to be confused with a spread contract).
  \item[Limit order and fill.] A limit order specifies the highest price a
        buyer will pay or the lowest price a seller will accept. A \emph{fill}
        is an execution of some or all of that order. An order that remains
        available for others to trade against is a \emph{resting order}.
  \item[Last trade (last print).] The price of the most recent completed trade.
        It can be old: if nobody has traded for a minute, the last trade may no
        longer reflect where the bid and ask are.
  \item[Maker and taker.] A \emph{maker} supplies a resting quote; a
        \emph{taker} executes against one. A limit order can act in either
        role, depending on whether it trades immediately or rests first.
  \item[Long and short positions.] A \emph{long} position benefits from a
        price increase; a \emph{short} position benefits from a decrease.
        In this thesis, buying YES opens a long position and selling YES
        opens a short position. Closing a position reverses that trade.
  \item[Settlement.] The exchange resolves the event and pays the contract's
        final value. A YES contract settles at 100 cents if its condition is
        met and zero otherwise.
  \item[Profit factor.] Total profit from profitable trades divided by the
        absolute total loss from losing trades. A value above one indicates
        that aggregate profits exceed aggregate losses.
  \item[Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown.] The Sharpe ratio compares average
        profit or return with its variability; the observation interval and
        annualisation assumptions must be specified. Maximum drawdown is the
        largest decline in cumulative profit or portfolio value from a
        preceding peak.
\end{description}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\section{Background}
Prediction markets are exchanges where each contract pays \$1 if a stated event
occurs and \$0 otherwise, so a price of 63 cents is read as a 63\% chance
\citep{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise}. Regulated and crypto-based venues
such as Kalshi and Polymarket now list thousands of contracts on economic data,
weather, politics, sports and many other events. Sports account for a large share
of the activity. Pew Research counts sports as 80\% of all trading volume on Kalshi
and 39\% on Polymarket between July 2024 and April 2026 \citep{radde2026trading}.
During the 2026 FIFA World Cup, the combined monthly volume of the two venues rose
from \$26 billion in May to \$53 billion in July, and sports trading in June and
July alone reached \$58 billion on Kalshi and \$22 billion on Polymarket
\citep{radde2026doubled}.
These figures measure notional taker volume, counting each traded contract at its
\$1 payout value rather than its transaction price.

Machine learning is now widely applied to financial markets. Models trained on
firm characteristics and past prices are used to forecast asset returns and
outperform linear benchmarks \citep{gu2020empirical}, and recurrent networks built
on the long short-term memory and gated recurrent unit designs read sequences of
prices to predict the direction of the next move
\citep{fischer2018deep,cho2014learning}. The same tools have reached betting and
prediction markets, where models forecast the outcome of sports games and are
compared with bookmaker odds \citep{bunker2019machine,galekwa2024systematic}.
Sports prediction markets have a structure that is useful for forecasting.
A game is traded through several
contracts that settle on the same final result, with one contract for each team to
win and ladders of contracts for the margin of victory (spreads) and the combined
score (totals). The contracts are also tied to an event that is played out in front
of the market. While the game goes on, its score, the time left and the field
position change play after play, and the prices of its contracts change with them.

These markets therefore offer two sources of predictive information: the sequence
of plays within a game and the relationships among contracts on its final score.
This motivates a model that combines a recurrent representation of play-by-play
data with message passing between contracts, allowing each contract's forecast to
use information from the others.

\section{Motivation}
Prediction-market prices are widely regarded as among the best available measures
of the probability of an event, because they combine the information and beliefs of
many traders who stand to lose money if they are wrong
\citep{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise}. Evidence of slow information
diffusion in equity markets motivates asking whether similar delays occur in
sports prediction markets \citep{hong2000bad}.

Live sports provide a setting in which observable events repeatedly update the
information available to traders. A scoring play, for example, can change the
probabilities of several related contract outcomes. This makes it possible to
examine whether game-state information and related contract prices predict
subsequent price changes beyond a forecast of no change. Whether such
predictability exists, and how long it persists, bears on how promptly these
markets incorporate information.

Predictability alone does not establish a trading opportunity. A contract's last
trade can remain stale after its bid and ask have already adjusted, so predicting
the next traded price need not identify a price at which a trader can transact.
Any apparent advantage must also survive fees, feed delay and the possibility
that a limit order is never filled. This study therefore examines both forecasting
performance and the execution conditions under which a forecast could be traded.

\section{Problem Statement}
This study asks whether a learned model can predict short-horizon price
changes in sports prediction markets well enough to earn a profit after trading
costs. Addressing this question requires a forecasting target, a way to combine
the available information, and an evaluation that separates predictive skill
from simulated trading gains.

First, the task differs from final-outcome prediction. Much of the reviewed
machine-learning literature on sports betting forecasts game results and compares
them with bookmaker odds
\citep{bunker2019machine,galekwa2024systematic,hubacek2019exploiting}.
Studies of prediction-market calibration instead ask whether observed outcome
frequencies agree with price levels \citep{burgi2025makers,moshrefi2026prices}.
Both inform betting decisions, but neither directly measures the ability to
forecast a contract's price change over the next 30 to 120 seconds. Work on live
betting also examines how stakes respond to match events
\citep{otting2022reaction}; the question here concerns subsequent traded prices
and returns after execution costs.

Second, the information has both temporal and relational structure. A forecast
can use the plays observed so far, the current game state and the prices of other
contracts that depend on the same final score. Whether recurrent play memory and
explicit information sharing between contracts improve forecasts is a modeling
question to be tested through ablation, rather than assumed from the market's
structure.

Third, forecast evaluation and trading evaluation face different sources of
optimism. Repeated strategy selection can inflate reported returns
\citep{bailey2014pseudo,lopezdeprado2018advances}. In these markets, stale
reference prices and uncertain limit-order fills create additional difficulties.
Historical inputs must respect their assumed availability times, and profitability
must be assessed separately from accuracy under stated fee and fill assumptions.

The task is therefore to forecast short-horizon changes in traded prices using
in-game information and related contract prices, and to assess whether these
forecasts support profitable trading under explicit execution assumptions.

\section{Research Objectives}
The aim is to build a forecaster of short-horizon price changes in sports
prediction markets and to evaluate it under explicit information-availability and
execution assumptions. This breaks down
into four objectives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Develop an in-game forecaster that combines play-by-play data with market
        prices to predict contract-price changes at 30, 60 and 120 seconds
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:ingame});
  \item Quantify the contribution of model components and inputs through ablation,
        and assess sensitivity to feed delay and trading rules
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments});
  \item Evaluate forecasting and trading performance using an as-of protocol,
        walk-forward folds, a later out-of-sample period with documented protocol revisions,
        and explicit fee and fill assumptions
        (Chapters~\ref{ch:data} and~\ref{ch:experiments});
  \item Compare historical simulation with live paper trading to identify
        differences associated with feed availability and execution
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:trading}).
\end{itemize}

The study uses Kalshi as its only trading venue. Its accessible trade history,
order book and fee schedule support historical analysis and live paper trading,
subject to assumptions about historical quote availability and execution. It covers
Major League Baseball (MLB), the National Basketball Association (NBA) and the
National Football League (NFL). The model forecasts winner, spread and total
contracts, while the trading evaluation is restricted to spreads and totals.
Evaluation consists of historical simulation and live paper trading, with no real
orders submitted.

\section{Research Approach}\label{sec:approach}
The study asks two questions in turn. Does the forecaster predict short-horizon
price changes better than assuming no change? And, if it does, does that forecast
survive fees, order fills and the delay with which live feeds report plays? The
first is a question about forecasting accuracy, the second about trading, and
they are evaluated separately because an accurate forecast need not be tradable.

The evidence is built in three stages (Table~\ref{tab:approach}). Walk-forward
forecasts use games later than each model's training sample, but their results
also inform model and strategy selection. A later out-of-sample period tests
transfer beyond development, subject to the protocol changes disclosed in
Section~\ref{sec:data-splits}. Live paper trading and order-book simulation
then examine the strategy under observed feed arrivals and liquidity. Each
stage applies the as-of rule: a forecast uses only information available at
the decision time. Chapter~\ref{ch:data} gives the full protocol.

\section{Main Contributions}
This work makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item An in-game forecasting model that combines recurrent play-by-play
        representations with message passing among winner, spread and total
        contracts to predict price changes over 30--120 seconds.
  \item An empirical analysis of the sources of short-horizon predictability
        across three sports. Component and input ablations examine the value of
        structural price references and contract message passing, while finding
        little additional benefit from play-type labels given the remaining
        inputs. Feed-delay experiments characterize how forecasting and simulated
        trading performance respond to information latency under the stated
        decision rules.
  \item A combined historical and live paper-trading evaluation that identifies
        practical limits to converting forecasts into trading returns. It combines
        an as-of protocol, later out-of-sample data and live observations to document
        discrepancies involving stale reference prices, feed arrival times and
        order execution, and assesses their implications for interpreting
        simulated profitability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Results}
On the walk-forward folds, the forecaster improves on predicting no price change
and produces positive simulated returns after modeled fees across the six spread
and total contract types. These folds also informed the trading rule and sizing,
so their returns are development results rather than an independent test of the
chosen strategy. Ablations indicate that structural price inputs are particularly
valuable, message passing improves forecast skill across all six contract types,
and play-type labels add little.

At the two-minute horizon, development forecast skill ranges from 4.5\% to
7.9\% across the six spread and total groups. In the later out-of-sample period,
baseball earns 5.4 and 5.0 cents per contract on spreads and totals respectively;
football earns 2.6 and 0.1 cents under its revised decision interval. The
forward evaluation covers \resmainMLBAllgames{} MLB, \resmainNBAAllgames{} NBA
and \resmainNFLAllgames{} NFL games, with positive net returns in four of six
contract groups. MLB totals and NBA totals lose money. These observations
support some transfer of the selected signals while showing that feed
availability and execution remain important. Detailed uncertainty measures and the
protocol qualifications accompany the results in
Chapters~\ref{ch:experiments} and~\ref{ch:trading}.

\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:background}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
This chapter reviews four themes: calibration and information arrival in
prediction markets, sports outcome and in-game probability models, temporal and
relational forecasting, and the evaluation of forecasts and trading strategies.
A final synthesis identifies the questions addressed by this study.

\section{Calibration and Information in Prediction Markets}\label{sec:efficiency}
\paragraph{Prices as probabilities.}
Prediction markets aggregate information through trading, so their prices are
often interpreted as forecasts of event probabilities
\citep{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise}. This interpretation can be assessed
by comparing prices with observed outcomes: among contracts trading at 20 cents,
roughly 20\% should eventually pay out. Such agreement is called calibration.
Calibration across a sample does not, however, establish how quickly prices
respond to individual events during a game.

One departure from calibration is the favourite--longshot bias, in which longshots
win less often than their prices imply. In horse-race betting,
\citet{snowberg2010explaining} use compound bets to distinguish probability
misperceptions from risk-loving preferences, finding stronger support for
misperceptions as an explanation of longshot overbetting.
On Kalshi, \citet{burgi2025makers} relate favourite--longshot patterns to fees
and maker--taker participation, showing that observed returns also depend on
how traders enter the market.
For sports moneyline contracts, \citet{moshrefi2026prices} finds that calibration
varies with time to expiry, with sharper departures near settlement. These
results concern agreement with settlement outcomes, a different property from
the speed at which new information enters prices.

\paragraph{Reactions to information.}
Across sports, discrete game events allow researchers to examine how quickly
prices incorporate news. In association football, \citet{gil2007testing} report
mixed evidence from the 2002 World Cup, including continued price trends after
goals. Such drift can also reflect changing outcome probabilities as game time
passes. \citet{croxson2014information} isolate goal news from this continuing
information flow by examining goals near half-time and find swift, full price
adjustment. Their comparison shows why post-event drift alone need not imply
an exploitable delay.

Adjustment may also differ across contracts on the same game.
\citet{brown2014information} finds temporary mispricing between related in-play
tennis assets, partly associated with differences in their price-update
frequencies. This motivates examining whether related sports contracts help
forecast one another's subsequent price movements, even when they respond to
the same underlying news.

An apparent delay can, however, arise from infrequent trading.
\citet{hasbrouck1991measuring} models trades and quote revisions jointly to
estimate the information content and lasting price impact of trades. In sparse
sports markets, a later print may simply catch up with quotes that have already
incorporated the news. Forecasting that print does not establish an executable
opportunity; the relevant check is whether the predicted movement remains
tradable at the available quotes.

\section{Sports Prediction and In-Game Probabilities}
\paragraph{Outcome prediction.}
Reviews by \citet{bunker2019machine} and \citet{galekwa2024systematic} cover neural
networks, statistical classifiers and ensemble methods for sports prediction and
betting. Bunker and Susnjak emphasise feature engineering, comparison across
model families and the difficulty of comparing results obtained from different
datasets and competitions. Their findings support evaluating both input
representations and architecture within a common forecasting task.

For betting, a likely winner is attractive only at odds offering a positive
expected return. \citet{hubacek2019exploiting} incorporate this distinction into
an NBA forecasting system by reducing correlation with bookmaker forecasts and
allocating bets through a portfolio-based rule. A model that mainly reproduces
the bookmaker's predictions offers little basis for identifying favourable odds.
\citet{walsh2024machine} likewise find that calibration-based model selection
produces better NBA betting returns than accuracy-based selection. Correctly
ranking the likely winner is insufficient when betting decisions depend on the
size of the probability difference from market odds.

\paragraph{In-game probability models.}
In-game models update outcome probabilities as play progresses.
\citet{pelechrinis2017iwinrnfl} develop a logistic regression model of NFL win
probability from play-by-play variables, reporting well-calibrated probabilities
without significant improvement from the more complex nonlinear models considered.
This suggests that much of the predictive information may already be captured by
current-state variables. In soccer, \citet{robberechts2021bayesian} estimate
remaining goals through time-varying scoring rates conditioned on game context,
then obtain win, draw and loss probabilities. Their approach makes the scoring
process explicit, which is important when a single goal can substantially change
the outcome. These models estimate fundamental game uncertainty; the
event-response literature instead asks how market prices incorporate it.

Distributional models also connect game state to related contract outcomes.
\citet{stern1994brownian} models basketball score differences using Brownian
motion and derives win probabilities conditional on the lead and elapsed time.
A distribution of the final margin can provide probabilities for both winning
and covering a spread; totals require a distribution of the combined score.
This motivates the structural reference used here, which combines game-state
and market information under explicit distributional assumptions. Its
construction is described in Chapter~\ref{ch:ingame}.

\section{Temporal and Relational Forecasting Models}
\paragraph{Sequence and graph representations.}
In-game forecasting can draw on both the current state and the sequence of events
that produced it. The gated recurrent unit of \citet{cho2014learning} updates a
hidden representation through gates that retain previous information or replace
it with new information. This allows a play history to be summarised as the game
progresses. A GRU is a practical recurrent encoder here; the literature does not
establish it as preferable to an LSTM or transformer for this task. Its predictive
value depends on whether history adds information beyond the current score,
clock and other state variables.

A complementary source of information lies in the relationships between
contracts. Since winner, spread and total contracts settle on the same game,
their prices may help inform one another's forecasts. Message-passing networks
provide a framework for incorporating these relationships by updating each
node's representation with information aggregated from its neighbours
\citep{gilmer2017neural}. The graph specifies which contracts exchange
information, while learned transformations allow their influence to vary with
contract features. This accommodates differences in how contracts respond to
the same play.

\paragraph{Temporal graph forecasting.}
Temporal graph models address forecasting problems in which related series
evolve together. DCRNN combines recurrent modelling with a directed road graph,
allowing traffic forecasts to use connected sensors \citep{li2018diffusion}.
Graph WaveNet and AGCRN instead learn additional or alternative connections
from data \citep{wu2019graph,bai2020adaptive}. The distinction concerns whether
relationships are supplied from domain knowledge or estimated during training.
In finance, \citet{luo2024spatial} derive a company graph from financial-news
co-occurrences, allowing a stock forecast to draw on related companies. These
statistical links are a weaker analogue than the shared settlement variables of
sports contracts. Brown's evidence of uneven adjustment across related betting
assets supplies the more direct economic motivation; graph forecasting supplies
a method for learning how information is combined across those assets.

\paragraph{What architecture comparisons establish.}
Sequence memory and cross-contract aggregation address different hypotheses:
whether play history adds information beyond the current state, and whether
other contracts add information beyond a contract's own features. Neither
hypothesis requires assuming that a neural model is superior. The component
ablations in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} test these contributions within the
chosen model, with forecast skill measured against no change. They do not
establish superiority over other nonlinear model families, which would require
matched comparisons on the same forecasting task. The detailed
architecture is presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:ingame}.

\section{Evaluating Forecasts and Trading Strategies}
\paragraph{Forecast metrics and temporal validation.}
The distinction between forecasting outcomes and trading price movements also
shapes evaluation. For outcome probabilities, strictly proper scoring rules,
including logarithmic and Brier scores, are optimized in expectation by reporting
the true probabilities \citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Unlike classification
accuracy, they distinguish between forecasts that select the same winner but
assign different probabilities. For price changes, squared error evaluates the
conditional-mean forecast. Benchmark-relative forecast skill compares its error
with that of a simple reference, as in the out-of-sample evaluation of
\citet{campbell2008predicting}. Here the reference is zero change, rather than
their historical-average return: it asks whether a forecast improves on retaining
the current price. The resulting $R^2_{\mathrm{NC}}$ is a squared-error skill
score, not conventional mean-centred $R^2$. Predictive skill alone does not
account for the prices and quantities available for execution.

The reliability of historical returns also depends on how the strategy was
selected. Comparing many variants increases the chance of selecting one that
performed well through sampling noise, even when each variant uses chronological
splits \citep{bailey2014pseudo,lopezdeprado2018advances}. Results used to choose
an architecture, trading threshold or sizing rule therefore form part of
strategy development. A separate out-of-sample period
provides stronger evidence when its protocol is fixed before the results are
examined. In live sports, this temporal discipline extends to feed availability:
an archived play timestamp may precede the time at which its information
actually reached a trader.

Dependence between observations further affects the interpretation of results.
Forecasts at overlapping horizons share future prices, and contracts on the
same game share information and outcomes. Treating these observations as
independent can overstate precision. Cluster-based inference permits dependence
within groups, provided assumptions about the independence of clusters are
credible \citep{cameron2015practitioner}. Aggregating returns or paired model
differences by game addresses within-game dependence; it does not eliminate
dependence between simultaneous games. Resampling whole games, or date blocks
when shared conditions matter, preserves more of this dependence than resampling
individual forecast rows. The implemented uncertainty measures are specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.

\paragraph{Maker fees, fills and adverse selection.}
Execution costs matter when forecast movements are only a few cents. Kalshi
distinguishes taker orders executing against existing quotes from maker orders
resting on the book; a limit order can execute in either role
\citep{kalshi2026feeshelp}. \citet{burgi2025makers} find higher average returns
after fees for makers than takers, although both groups have negative average
returns in their sample. Providing liquidity is consequently not universally
profitable. \citet{avellaneda2008high} formalise the trade-off between quote
placement, execution intensity and inventory risk. Their market-making model
provides context for passive execution, rather than a direct model of a
directional sports strategy or empirical validation of its fills.

Execution also exposes traders to adverse selection. \citet{glosten1985bid}
show how better-informed traders selectively accept favourable quotes, imposing
expected losses on liquidity providers. In a live game, a resting buy order may
be filled after news has reduced the contract's value, before it is cancelled
or repriced. \citet{lo2002econometric} estimate limit-order execution times from
actual order data using survival analysis, illustrating why execution probability
is an empirical object rather than an automatic consequence of a favourable
forecast. Historical prints alone do not reveal a hypothetical order's queue
position or guarantee its fill. Evaluation must account for both the orders that
execute and the returns conditional on execution.

\paragraph{Sizing and risk.}
Sizing can change trading returns even when forecasts and entry rules are held
fixed. \citet{kelly1956new} derives allocation by maximising expected logarithmic
wealth growth; applying that principle requires a distribution of payoffs and
attention to simultaneous exposures. Contracts on one game share risk, so
independent contract-level sizing can concentrate exposure. For a strategy that
exits before settlement, the relevant payoff distribution includes exit prices,
fees and execution uncertainty. A Kelly fraction based solely on final win
probability is consequently not justified by a short-horizon price-change
forecast. Empirical sizing rules require the same evaluation discipline as
entry thresholds.

\section{Synthesis and Research Gap}
The literature distinguishes estimating game uncertainty, forecasting market
adjustment and demonstrating an executable return. This study brings those
questions together for related contracts on the same sporting event.

The reviewed work leaves open whether jointly modelling Kalshi winner, spread
and total contracts improves short-horizon traded-price forecasts, and whether
any gains survive information delays, executable-price checks, fees and
conditional fills. Our contribution is an empirical test of these questions
across three sports, with component ablations separating play memory from
cross-contract aggregation. Predictability of a stale last print is insufficient
evidence of a tradable lag; the economic conclusion depends on execution
evidence as well as the forecast score.

\section{Market Setting and Contract Universe}\label{sec:kalshi}
The study covers game-level contracts on MLB, NBA and NFL games traded on Kalshi.
Each contract has YES and NO outcomes, with a correct position paying \$1 at
settlement. Prices are expressed in cents throughout the analysis. Winner
contracts concern the winning team, spread contracts concern a team's margin of
victory, and total contracts concern the combined score. Different thresholds
produce ladders of related contracts on the same game.

The forecasting universe comprises winner, spread and total contracts, which
share game-level information and offer usable market coverage for joint
modelling. Other markets were excluded because of limited quote coverage,
additional feature requirements, or weak forecasting and trading potential in
preliminary analysis. 

Games enter the development panel when they have matched play-by-play data,
a winner contract, and both spread and total ladders. Only regular-season and
playoff games are included; preseason and exhibition games, including MLB spring
training, NBA preseason and NBA Summer League, are excluded.

The historical analysis uses individual trade records and one-minute bid and ask
candlesticks because continuous order-book records are unavailable for this sample
\citep{kalshi2026trades,kalshi2026candles}. Trades identify the initiating side:
a YES-taker print provides an ask-side observation, while a NO-taker print
provides a bid-side observation when expressed as a YES price. These observations
supplement the candlesticks between minute updates, but cannot recover continuous
quotes, available depth or queue positions. This data choice supports price
forecasting while limiting the precision of historical execution estimates;
fills must be assessed under the assumptions in Section~\ref{sec:execution}.

Contract outcomes are taken from Kalshi's settlement labels. The game's final
score provides an integrity check, while its play-by-play end time determines
the end of the in-game observation period. Market closure follows the exchange's
own schedule and can occur before or after the game ends, so using it as the
game-end timestamp could truncate play or include post-game observations as
in-game data. The pipeline therefore uses the sports feed to identify game end
and retains the exchange close time as a diagnostic. The feed-based boundary
also identifies forecast horizons extending beyond the game, for which missing
future trades may be replaced by settlement payoffs as described below.

\section{Forecasting Task}\label{sec:task}
The task is to forecast traded-price changes in winner, spread and total
contracts on MLB, NBA and NFL games. For game $g$, let $C_g(t)$ be its set of
contracts at decision time $t$, and let $X_g(t)$ contain the play history, game
state and market observations available by that time. The forecasting problem is
\begin{align}
  \widehat\Delta_i^{(h)}(t) &= f_\theta\bigl(X_g(t),i,h\bigr),
  && i\in C_g(t),\quad h\in\{30,60,120\},
  \label{eq:forecast-mapping}\\
  \Delta_i^{(h)}(t) &= \bar p_{i,h}(t)-a_i(t),
  \label{eq:forecast-target}
\end{align}
where $a_i(t)$ is the reference price at decision time and $\bar p_{i,h}(t)$ is
the representative future traded price defined below. Both prices and their
difference are measured in cents; horizons are in seconds. The model parameters
$\theta$ are learned from the training data. Inputs use only information available
at $t$, while future prices supply the targets. The horizons are
30, 60 and 120 seconds, with the 120-second forecast used for trading.
Figure~\ref{fig:forecast-timeline} illustrates the timing of inputs and targets.

\paragraph{Decision times and inputs.}
The play unit is one pitch in MLB, one possession in NBA and one play from
scrimmage in NFL. Each game is sampled on a five-second grid. At time $t$, the model receives the
play history available under the specified feed delay, the corresponding game
state, and observations of the game's contracts available by that time. Play
features include event type, score changes and feed-provided win probabilities;
contract features include prices, quote age and recent trading activity. The
structural price reference is computed from the available game state and market
information. Chapter~\ref{ch:ingame} describes these representations and how they
are combined.

For contract $i$, let $a_i(t)$ denote the price from which the forecast change is
measured. In the traded-price panel, this anchor is the last trade no more than
120 seconds old, with a reconstructed quote-based value used when that trade is
unavailable. The separate trading rule requires a print within the previous
60 seconds before considering an entry. Forecasting rows and trading decisions
therefore have different eligibility conditions.

\paragraph{Future-price targets.}
Trading is irregular, so an exact transaction price need not exist at $t+h$.
Instead, the target uses the trades in the following 15-second window,
\begin{equation}
  J_{i,t,h}=\{j: t+h < t_{ij}\leq t+h+15\},
\end{equation}
where $t_{ij}$ is the timestamp of trade $j$ in contract $i$. If $p_{ij}$ is its
YES price and $v_{ij}$ its size, the representative future price is
\begin{equation}
  \bar p_{i,h}(t)=
  \frac{\sum_{j\in J_{i,t,h}}v_{ij}p_{ij}}
       {\sum_{j\in J_{i,t,h}}v_{ij}}.
\end{equation}
The minimum and maximum prices in the same window define an interval used by
the training loss. Forecast accuracy is evaluated against the volume-weighted
price change defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:forecast-target}.

An empty target window is marked missing and does not contribute to that
horizon's price score or loss. Where a horizon extends beyond the feed's end of
game and there is no target-window trade, the panel substitutes the contract's
settlement payoff. These observations concern terminal value rather than a
subsequent execution price. The model also has an auxiliary binary target
$y_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicating whether the contract settles YES. Price-change
forecasts drive the trading decisions in Chapter~\ref{ch:trading}; the settlement
output supports training through the auxiliary objective in
Section~\ref{sec:ingame-loss}. Future prices and outcomes are labels and do not
enter the input history.

\section{Data Construction and Information Availability}\label{sec:asof}
\paragraph{Matching and market observations.}
Contracts are matched to games using league, teams and date, with explicit
handling of relistings and rescheduled games. Trades are deduplicated by trade
identifier. Prints sharing a timestamp use a consistent ordering to approximate
the sequence of a sweep; their exact execution order is not observed.

Quote observations combine candlestick closes with transaction-implied bid or
ask observations. Candles enter only at the end of their completed minute;
trades enter at their recorded creation time. Each side retains its observation
time, so staleness and missing sides can be represented in the inputs. A carried
quote describes the latest observed price, not a guarantee that the price
remained executable until the next observation.

A contract-step row requires a reference price, but incomplete quotes do not
automatically exclude it. Quote availability, age and width remain visible
to the model, allowing it to distinguish reliable observations from stale
or one-sided markets. The precise retention rules are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:panel-conventions}.

\paragraph{Play timing and feed delay.}
The model must observe a play's outcome after it becomes available, rather than
at the start of an action whose result is still unknown. MLB inputs use each
pitch's result time, with plate-appearance win probabilities revealed only on
the final pitch of that appearance. An explicit timing approximation makes a
home-run result available no later than seven seconds after pitch start when
the archived end timestamp includes the subsequent trot. This is a modeling
assumption, not a measured feed-arrival time.

NBA events are grouped into possessions, including the associated free-throw
sequence, and become available at the possession's end. NFL plays use their
nflverse end timestamp when matched; missing end times are approximated from
snap time and the median duration of that play type in earlier games. These
rules avoid exposing a completed play's score or win probability at its start.

Let $\tau_j$ be the modeled result time of play $j$ and $d$ the assumed feed
delay. At decision time $t$, the historical model can use only plays satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \tau_j+d\leq t.
\end{equation}
Plays are ordered by availability, and game-state features are rebuilt from the
available prefix. The delay therefore affects the play memory, score, clock and
state-derived contract features together. Market observations retain their own
timestamps. The delay is set per sport from the latency of the live feed, as
described next; alternative delays are assessed separately.

\paragraph{Latency requirements and the choice of delay.}\label{sec:data-latency}
A model is only usable in trading if it can be run on information that has
actually arrived. The public play-by-play feeds used for live trading do not
publish a play at the moment it happens, and the Kalshi market prices a play
within roughly 5 to 20 seconds. Measured on the recorded live feeds, plays reach us
about 7 to 15 seconds after the pitch in MLB, a median of about 45 seconds after the
event in NBA (ESPN updates in batches roughly every 20 seconds, with a 90th
percentile near 75 seconds) and about 43 seconds in NFL. The model for each sport
is therefore trained and evaluated with a delay that its live feed can meet:
10 seconds for MLB, which covers 97\% of observed arrivals (53\% at 5 seconds),
and 60 seconds for NBA and NFL, which exceeds the typical arrival time with room
for batching. A model that used a play before the feed delivers it would show a
forecast skill and profit that no live runner could reproduce.

\paragraph{Preventing look-ahead bias.}
Look-ahead bias arises when a historical forecast uses information that would
not have been available at the decision time, which inflates retrospective
performance. The as-of principle guards against this: inputs and trading
decisions at $t$ use only information available by $t$. Historical joins take
the latest observation at or before the decision time, allowing for the feed
delay and the completion time of candlesticks. Future trade prices and
settlement outcomes serve only as labels, and final listing fields such as
lifetime volume are kept out of the inputs. Missing values are masked, input
scales are fixed per league, and later out-of-sample data are never used to fit parameters or
select stopping epochs.

These controls cannot show that the historical information set exactly matches
what a live trader saw. Archived play-by-play generally holds the final version
of each event, so later corrections may remain even after a fixed delay, which
approximates availability rather than reconstructing every revision and
delivery time. Recorded live feeds provide a separate check on this remaining
risk.

\paragraph{Data-quality checks.}\label{sec:data-quirks}
Checks cover event ordering, contract matching, duplicate keys and consistency
between settlement labels and final scores.
Postponed games and doubleheaders are checked against the specific fixture
attached to each listing. Games are assigned to evaluation periods by their
first-play time.

seeds are used, with their forecasts averaged for the trading evaluation.

\section{Evaluation Measures}\label{sec:data-metrics}
\paragraph{Forecast skill.}
Since the naive forecast of a traded price is its last value, the natural
benchmark for a price-change forecast is zero change. For observations with an
available target, forecast skill is the proportional reduction in squared error
relative to that benchmark, an out-of-sample $R^2$ centred on zero rather than on
the sample mean:
\begin{equation}
 R^2_{\mathrm{NC}}=
 1-\frac{\sum_{(i,t)}\left(\Delta_i^{(h)}(t)-\widehat\Delta_i^{(h)}(t)\right)^2}
          {\sum_{(i,t)}\left(\Delta_i^{(h)}(t)\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
A value of zero means the model is no better than retaining the anchor price;
positive values mean it is better and negative values worse. Scores are
reported by league, contract type and horizon. Training also uses the observed
price interval, while the auxiliary outcome head is assessed using probabilistic
loss. The forecasting loss and interval score are defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:ingame}.

\paragraph{Trading performance and uncertainty.}
Trading results report profit before fees and after the assumed fees. If trade $j$ has size $q_j$ and net profit $r_j$ cents per contract,
the size-weighted net return is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Net\ cents/contract}=\frac{\sum_j q_j r_j}{\sum_j q_j}.
\end{equation}
Total profit and traded quantity are reported alongside this ratio, so a result
based on very little trading remains visible. Returns are aggregated by game
for uncertainty assessment; ablation comparisons use paired differences in
per-game profit. $t$-statistics are computed over game-level profits (one observation 
per game). Shared market conditions across games can still create dependence, and 
the many model and strategy comparisons limit the interpretation of isolated 
significance levels. Main results and sensitivity analyses are presented in 
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}.

\section{Model Overview}

three sources of information: the sequence of observed plays, the current game
state, and prices and activity across contracts on the same game. A recurrent
encoder summarises play history, and a graph network exchanges information
between contract types. Two output heads then produce price-change forecasts
and an auxiliary settlement-probability estimate.

Figure~\ref{fig:ingame-arch} shows the computation at one decision time. Each

The same architecture is used across MLB, NBA and NFL, with separate models
trained for each sport. Building on the data construction in
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, this chapter explains how the inputs become a shared game
context, how related contracts exchange information, and how the resulting
representations are trained to forecast prices.

\section{Input Representations and Structural Reference}\label{sec:ingame-inputs}
\subsection{Play, Game and Contract Features}
Inputs are organised into a sequence of play vectors, a shared game-state
vector at each decision time, and a vector for each contract. All follow the
information-availability rules in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}. Table~\ref{tab:input-encoding}
summarises their numerical representation; the learned transformations of these
vectors are described in Section~\ref{sec:ingame-arch}.

\paragraph{Play sequence.}
The play unit is a pitch in MLB, a possession in NBA and a play from scrimmage
in NFL. Event types use a fixed set of sport-specific indicators. Each vector
contains both the state after the
play and changes caused by it: score margin and combined score describe the
state, while changes in these quantities describe the scoring event. Possession
is represented by separate home and away indicators. Additional situation
features include down, distance and field position in NFL, and outs, balls and
strikes in MLB. Remaining regulation time is represented as a fraction.

Numerical input features are normalised using fixed sport-specific scales to
keep their magnitudes comparable. Time gaps use logarithmic transforms to
reduce the influence of long stoppages, while win probabilities remain on a
zero-to-one scale. The same transformations are applied during training and
evaluation; missing-value conventions are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\paragraph{Shared game context.}
At each decision time, the current available state supplies the remaining
fraction, scaled score margin and total, and possession indicators. It also
records whether a new play became available during the preceding five seconds
and the time since the latest available play. Market inputs include the
normalised home-winner price, an availability indicator and the logarithm
of recent winner-market trading activity. This separates the current state
from the history represented by the recurrent encoder.

\paragraph{Contract representation.}
Winner, spread and total contracts use three type indicators; two further
indicators identify the home or away side, both zero for totals. Quote and
trade prices are normalised to a zero-to-one scale. Ages and volumes use
logarithmic transforms, while signed trade flow is logarithmically transformed
with its direction preserved. Threshold distance is $k-s_iM(t)$ for spreads and $k-S(t)$ for
totals, where $M(t)$ is the home-minus-away margin and $S(t)$ the combined score;
the distance is normalised consistently with the score margin. The structural
reference and its gap from the anchor, $b_i(t)-a_i(t)$, are also normalised.

Availability indicators distinguish observed prices from missing values and
identify recent trades and usable structural references. The network can
therefore interpret a price alongside its freshness and coverage.

\subsection{Structural Price Reference}\label{sec:structural-reference}
The features above describe the game state and the market observations for each
contract. Their implications for a particular contract also depend on its
settlement threshold: the same score and remaining time can make one total
likely to settle positively while leaving a higher total uncertain. A useful
additional input is therefore an approximate probability of exceeding each
threshold, expressed on the same scale as the observed contract price.

The structural price reference supplies this mapping through a simple scoring
distribution. For spreads, the current winner-market probability anchors a
distribution of the final score margin. For totals, the observed combined score
is supplemented by an estimate of scoring during the remaining game. These
references give the neural model an explicit relationship between game
conditions and contract thresholds; the gap from the observed price can then
inform its price-change forecast. The reference is an input feature, and the
model remains free to learn departures from it.

For spreads, following the normal-score approximation motivated by \citet{stern1994brownian},
let $\tau$ denote the fraction of regulation play remaining and $p_H$ the
home-win probability implied by the available winner-market price. Write
$z=\Phi^{-1}(p_H)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution
function. A normally distributed final home-minus-away margin with standard
deviation $\sigma_m\sqrt{\tau}$ and mean $\sigma_m\sqrt{\tau}\,z$ has home-win
probability $p_H$. The corresponding reference for a spread contract with threshold $k$ is
\begin{equation}
 b_i(t)=100\,\Phi\!\left(s_i z-\frac{k}{\sigma_m\sqrt{\tau}}\right),
 \qquad
 s_i=\begin{cases}1,&\text{home-team spread},\\-1,&\text{away-team spread}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:spread-reference}
\end{equation}
Here $k$ is the margin that the designated team must exceed. The current score
influences the spread reference through the winner-market probability; threshold
and remaining time determine how that probability maps to other spreads.

For an over-total contract, let $S(t)$ be the combined score and $L_g$ the
pregame total implied by the game's total ladder, interpolated at a 50-cent over
price. Approximating the remaining score by a normal distribution with mean
$L_g\tau$ and standard deviation $\sigma_T\sqrt{\tau}$ gives
\begin{equation}
 b_i(t)=100\,\Phi\!\left(
 \frac{S(t)+L_g\tau-k}{\sigma_T\sqrt{\tau}}\right).
 \label{eq:total-reference}
\end{equation}
The remaining fraction is based on the regulation clock in NBA and NFL, and on
outs remaining in MLB. Uncertainty decreases with the square root of this
fraction. The distribution widths are fixed sport-specific development choices;
their values and numerical safeguards are listed in
Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation}.

The assumptions deliberately simplify scoring: they do not model possession-
specific rates, discrete scores or every late-game effect. The model receives
both $b_i(t)$ and $b_i(t)-a_i(t)$ so that it can learn when the reference is
informative. No separate structural reference is assigned to winner contracts;
its absence is indicated explicitly. The contribution of these features is
assessed by ablation in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}.

\section{Temporal and Relational Architecture}\label{sec:ingame-arch}
The encoded inputs enter two complementary stages. Play memory represents the
history of the game, while message passing combines the current observations
across its contracts. Both stages condition the forecast for each contract.

\subsection{Play Memory and Choice of GRU}
A GRU is used to maintain a fixed-size summary of the ordered play sequence.
Its gates allow earlier context to be retained or replaced as new plays arrive,
which suits a game observed through successive pitches, possessions or plays.
A single recurrent layer keeps this summary compact and makes it available to
all contracts on the game.

A preliminary comparison supported this choice: replacing the GRU with a
two-layer causal transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} reduced price-change
$R^2$ for spreads and totals at all three short horizons on the first three NFL
folds, with zero assumed feed delay. This limited development check supports
the choice of GRU for our purposes.

Let $u_j$ be the feature vector of the $j$th available play. A learned linear
projection and rectified linear activation precede a one-layer GRU
\citep{cho2014learning}:
\begin{equation}
 r_j=\operatorname{ReLU}(P u_j+b_P),\qquad
 h_j=\operatorname{GRU}(r_j,h_{j-1}),\qquad h_0=0.
\end{equation}
At decision time $t$, the model selects the state after the last available play,
$h_g(t)$. If no play is available, this state is zero. Thus the recurrent encoder
can represent patterns beyond the latest score and clock without requiring a
hand-written list of play sequences. Whether that memory improves forecasts is
tested by replacing it with zeros and retraining the remaining model.

The selected state conditions every contract on the game. Temporal memory
resides in this play encoder; graph representations are recomputed at each
decision time, allowing the contract set to change without maintaining a
separate recurrent state for each contract.

\subsection{Game Context and Contract Encoding}
Let $s_g(t)$ denote the current game and market features, and $x_i(t)$ the
features of contract $i$. The game context and initial node representation are
\begin{equation}
 c_g(t)=F_c\bigl([s_g(t),h_g(t)]\bigr),\qquad
 z_i^{(0)}=F_n\bigl(x_i(t)\bigr)+A c_g(t),
 \label{eq:node-encoding}
\end{equation}
where brackets denote concatenation. $F_c$ and $F_n$ are feed-forward networks,
and $A$ is a learned linear projection. Adding the shared context to each
contract encoding combines game-wide information with contract-specific prices
and thresholds. The hidden dimension is 64 throughout these representations.

\subsection{Message Passing Between Contract Types}
The initial node representations share a game context but still encode each
contract separately. Message passing lets a forecast also depend on the
observed state of related markets. Contracts on the game form the graph nodes.
Let $k_i$ be
the type of contract $i$ and $C_s$ the set of available nodes of type $s$, where
$s$ is winner, spread or total. In round $r$, the model first averages the node
representations within each sending type:
\begin{equation}
 \mu_s^{(r)}=\frac{1}{|C_s|}\sum_{j\in C_s}z_j^{(r)},\qquad
 m_i^{(r)}=\sum_s W_{k_i,s}^{(r)}\mu_s^{(r)}.
 \label{eq:contract-messages}
\end{equation}
An absent type contributes a zero vector. Each receiving--sending type pair has
its own learned matrix $W_{k_i,s}^{(r)}$. The averages include the receiving
contract's own type and representation.

Each node is then updated through a feed-forward transformation with a residual
connection:
\begin{equation}
 z_i^{(r+1)}=z_i^{(r)}+U_r\bigl([z_i^{(r)},m_i^{(r)}]\bigr),
 \qquad r=0,1.
 \label{eq:contract-update}
\end{equation}
The two rounds have separate parameters and operate on the same snapshot.
Connectivity is supplied through
shared game membership and contract types; the network learns the transformations
of aggregated information, rather than an adjacency matrix. Averaging within
types permits changing contract counts and threshold ladders across games.
Removing both rounds tests the value of explicit cross-contract aggregation.

\subsection{Forecast and Settlement Heads}
Both heads receive the final node representation and game context,
$v_i=[z_i^{(2)},c_g(t)]$. The price head produces one change in cents for each
configured horizon:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\Delta_i^{(h)}(t)=10\,D_h(v_i).
\end{equation}
The factor of ten is a fixed output scale, not a restriction on the predicted
change. The auxiliary head produces a log-odds adjustment $R(v_i)$. With
$p_i=\operatorname{clip}(a_i(t)/100,0.005,0.995)$, its settlement-probability
estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat q_i(t)=\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\left(
 \log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}+R(v_i)\right).
 \label{eq:settlement-head}
\end{equation}
A zero adjustment reproduces the clipped anchor probability. This auxiliary
task encourages the shared representation to retain information about final
outcomes, while trading decisions use the price-change forecasts.

\section{Training Objective and Procedure}\label{sec:ingame-training}
\subsection{Price and Auxiliary Losses}\label{sec:ingame-loss}
Trades within the target window can occur at different prices. Training
therefore uses the observed interval, avoiding a penalty for predictions that
fall within that range, and an auxiliary loss for the final outcome. Let
$\ell_i^{(h)}(t)$ and $u_i^{(h)}(t)$ be the minimum and maximum prices in the
15-second target window, and write
$\widehat p_i^{(h)}(t)=a_i(t)+\widehat\Delta_i^{(h)}(t)$. Its distance outside
the interval is
\begin{equation}
 e_i^{(h)}(t)=\max\!\left(\ell_i^{(h)}(t)-\widehat p_i^{(h)}(t),0\right)
             +\max\!\left(\widehat p_i^{(h)}(t)-u_i^{(h)}(t),0\right).
 \label{eq:interval-distance}
\end{equation}
This distance is zero inside the observed range. A Huber penalty limits the
influence of large deviations:
\begin{equation}
 H_\delta(e)=\begin{cases}
 \tfrac12 e^2,&0\leq e\leq\delta,\\
 \delta(e-\tfrac12\delta),&e>\delta,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad \delta=5\text{ cents}.
\end{equation}
Let $V$ index the valid contract--time--horizon targets in a batch and $B$ the
contract--time observations with a settlement label. The combined objective is
\begin{align}
 L_{\mathrm{price}}&=\frac{1}{10|V|}\sum_{(i,t,h)\in V}
                 H_\delta\bigl(e_i^{(h)}(t)\bigr),\\
 L_{\mathrm{result}}&=-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{(i,t)\in B}
  \left[y_i\log\widehat q_i(t)+(1-y_i)\log(1-\widehat q_i(t))\right],\\
 L&=L_{\mathrm{price}}+0.1L_{\mathrm{result}}.
 \label{eq:combined-loss}
\end{align}
Only valid targets contribute to each loss; padding and missing observations
are masked out according to Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.

The interval loss accommodates variation among trades within a target window;
it does not imply that every price in that interval was executable. Forecast
skill is evaluated against the volume-weighted price change rather than against
the interval alone. The interval formulation, auxiliary weight and Huber
threshold were set during development and were not separately ablated.

\subsection{Optimisation and Model Selection}
Separate models are trained for MLB, NBA and NFL using AdamW. Batches sample
decision times from multiple games, retaining all eligible contracts at each
sampled time so that message passing uses the complete available graph.
Padding masks accommodate differences in contract counts. Architecture and
optimisation settings are summarised in Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation}.

Model selection follows the chronological protocol in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}.
The latest 15\% of the available training games form the validation set. Early
stopping monitors the validation interval Huber loss, excluding the auxiliary
settlement loss, and the best checkpoint is restored for evaluation.

Each specification is trained with five random seeds, and its price-change
forecasts are averaged for trading evaluation. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}
first evaluates the complete model and its trading strategy, then uses
retrained ablations and sensitivity checks to examine the sources and limits
of the observed performance.

\section{Trading Strategy and Backtest Assumptions}\label{sec:execution}
Positive forecast skill does not establish tradability. Capturing a predicted
move requires an available entry, a subsequent exit and returns sufficient to
cover costs. The following rules define that conversion from forecasts to
simulated trades.
\subsection{Entry, Exit and Sizing Rules}
The reference strategy uses the 120-second forecast, with variants examined
later in the chapter. Its signal is the forecast price minus the latest traded
price. The entry and exit rules were fixed before inspecting the later
out-of-sample results except for the
NFL decision-interval change disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:data-splits}. The
current quantity scale is specified separately below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Decisions.} A decision is made every 5 seconds, with at most one
        open order or position per contract. A signal below 5 cents is ignored; this
        minimum is shared across leagues and contract types to limit further
        strategy-specific tuning.
  \item \textbf{Baseball skip window.} In baseball, no decision is made while 15 to
        60 seconds have passed since the last pitch the model knows of. After 15
        seconds with no new pitch, the next pitch has probably been thrown but, under
        the feed delay, is not yet known to the model, while the market has already
        priced it; an order placed then rests against participants who know the
        result. The window was chosen on the development folds and fixed before
        the later out-of-sample evaluation. Without it, net return on baseball falls from 9.8
        and 7.0 cents per contract on spreads and totals to 3.2 and 2.3 in the
        development folds, and from 5.4 and 5.0 to $-0.2$ and 0.6 on the later
        out-of-sample period.
  \item \textbf{Entry.} The order rests one cent inside the reference price, below it
        to buy and above it to sell, for up to 60 seconds.
  \item \textbf{Exit.} A fixed exit is attempted 120 seconds after entry
        (Section~\ref{sec:exec-tradeoffs}). Holding to settlement on the same entries
        is reported as a descriptive comparison only.
  \item \textbf{Size.} The signal and trading width determine quantity as
        specified in Section~\ref{sec:position-sizing}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Position Sizing and Trade Quantity}\label{sec:position-sizing}
Position sizing gives stronger forecasts more weight without assigning the
same quantity to every opportunity. For signal $s_j$ and observed trading width
$d_j$, both measured in cents, the normalised weight is
\begin{equation}
 w_j=\left[\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{|s_j|-5}{10}\right)\right]^2
 \ell_j,\qquad
 \ell_j=\begin{cases}
 \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}(d_j/10), & d_j\text{ observed},\\
 1, & \text{otherwise},
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}(x)=\min(1,\max(0,x))$.
The width is the difference between the latest YES- and NO-initiated trade
prices, requiring both trades to fall within the preceding 120 seconds.
The signal component rises quadratically above 5 cents and reaches its maximum
at 15 cents; the width component reaches its maximum at 10 cents. The quantity is
\begin{equation}
 q_j=\operatorname{round}_{0.01}(20w_j),
\end{equation}
using half-up rounding. The maximum order is therefore 20 contract units;
quantities that round to zero are omitted.

Table~\ref{tab:sizing-evidence} compares returns by signal strength before
applying a minimum signal or sizing weights. Each fill counts as one contract;
the execution rules and sport-specific feed delays are retained. Bands are
assigned after simulation, so they describe returns without selecting orders
by signal magnitude. Signals of at most 8 cents lose money in all six
league--contract groups, while signals of at least 15 cents earn the highest
mean return in each group. This pattern supports giving stronger signals more
weight, although it does not establish an optimal threshold or weighting
function. Returns are not uniformly monotonic, and the highest-signal NFL
spread and total bands contain only 11 and 7 fills, respectively.

The comparison does not show a consistent improvement in returns with

\subsection{Fee Structure and the Choice of Resting Orders}
Kalshi's fee schedule effective 7 July 2026 gives fees in dollars as
\begin{align}
  F_{\mathrm{taker}} &= R\!\left(0.07 M_t C p(1-p)\right), \\
  F_{\mathrm{maker}} &= R\!\left(0.0175 M_m C p(1-p)\right),
\end{align}
where $C$ is the number of contracts, $p$ is the dollar price, $M_t$ and $M_m$
are the respective series multipliers, and $R$ denotes the schedule's rounding
rule. The default multipliers are $M_t=1$ and $M_m=0$, with exceptions listed
by series \citep{kalshi2026feeschedule}.

At 50 cents and unit multipliers, the unrounded fees are 1.75 cents per contract
for a taker and 0.4375 cents for a maker. Two fills near that price therefore
cost approximately 3.5 cents if both take liquidity, compared with 0.875 cents
if both provide it, before rounding. This difference is material when the
forecast concerns a price movement of only a few cents.

The strategy consequently seeks entry through a resting limit order, quoting
one cent below the reference price to buy or one cent above it to sell. This
controls the entry price and aims to reduce fees and spread-crossing costs.
The reference is the last trade, however, so the order need not be competitive
with the current book. A favourable forecast does not imply that the desired
entry is available. Moreover, a limit that crosses the book executes as a taker;
the order-book-aware paper engine assigns the taker fee to such fills.

\subsection{Execution Trade-offs and Simulation Assumptions}\label{sec:exec-tradeoffs}
Resting orders exchange immediate execution for the possibility of a better
price and lower fees. An order may expire unfilled, or fill only after new
information makes its price unattractive. Adverse selection can therefore offset
the apparent saving \citep{glosten1985bid}. The choice of resting orders is a
cost-motivated design decision whose value must be assessed with the fill process,
rather than a claim that maker execution is always more profitable.

Under the specified strategy, entry orders rest for up to 60 seconds. Historical
simulation counts a fill only when a subsequent trade prints strictly through
the limit. The fixed-exit rule first attempts another resting order 120 seconds
after entry; an unsuccessful exit can fall back to taking liquidity or holding
to settlement. Maker execution at entry therefore does not guarantee maker fees
on the entire trade.

Historical backtests use a standardised fee model, deducting $1.75p(1-p)$ cents
per contract for maker fills and $7p(1-p)$ cents for taker fills, where $p$ is
the fill price in dollars. Fees are applied to both entry and exit according
to the execution type; positions held to settlement incur no exit trading fee.
All reported net returns include these costs. Chapter~\ref{ch:trading} uses
series-specific fees in the order-book replay.

These assumptions permit consistent historical comparisons but do not fully
reconstruct executable liquidity. Chapter~\ref{ch:trading} tests them against
recorded order books and live paper orders.

\subsection{Message Passing and Play Memory}
Message passing between the contracts of one game is the most valuable part of
the architecture. Without it, skill falls in all six contract types by 1.4 to 4.2
points, a loss of roughly 20 to 80\%, and profit falls significantly in three of
the six (baseball spreads and totals, and football totals). The effect on profit is
not significant in basketball and in football spreads. Without message passing the
model also trades 30 to 50\% less in baseball and about 40\% less in football,
while profit per contract is mixed: it falls from 9.8 to 6.0 cents for baseball
spreads and from 6.9 to 3.2 cents for football totals, and rises from 5.2 to
7.0 cents for football spreads.

This pattern is consistent with the shared settlement structure of contracts
on a game: neighbouring thresholds and related market types provide useful
context for an individual forecast. Play memory contributes less consistently.
Removing it lowers baseball skill by 0.5 and 1.4 points, but leaves basketball
and football skill unchanged or slightly higher (by up to 0.7 points), and its
profit effects are small except for baseball spreads ($t=-2.6$). The results
therefore support relational processing more strongly than a general claim
that longer play history improves trading returns.

\subsection{Structural Reference and Observed Inputs}
The largest skill losses arise when the structural-reference feature group is
removed. This group supplies threshold distance, the approximate price reference
and its gap from the anchor. Skill falls by 1.9 to 7.4 points in all six contract
types, and profit falls in every one, significantly in four ($t$ from $-2.8$ to
$-6.3$; basketball totals and football spreads reach only $-1.3$ and $-1.4$). The
removal turns baseball totals into a loss. For football, skill falls from about
7--9\% to about 1\%, and trading falls by 54 to 63\%. Removing win probability, which also
removes the reference features grouped with it, is nearly as costly, so the two effects cannot
be fully separated.

Event labels add little conditional on the retained game state. Removing play
type while keeping score, time and win probability changes skill by at most
0.4 points, with no clear profit improvement from keeping the labels. The
effects of plays on the score and win probability remain available, so this
does not imply that the plays themselves carry no information. Removing
recent trades or quotes costs 0.1 to 1.1 points of skill each. Both ablations
retain the reference price, measuring the additional value of trading
activity and quotes rather than the value of market information as a whole.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Taken together, the ablations indicate that the relationship between the game
state, contract thresholds and the last traded price is central to the
forecasts. They do not establish that the structural reference is a true fair
value or that the signal represents delayed adjustment to news. Skill and
profit usually agree, but not always: basketball spreads lose skill without message
passing yet keep their profit, football spreads earn more per contract without it,
and removing quotes raises baseball spread profit per contract while lowering skill. Profit depends on the few confident, fillable
signals, not on the average forecast, so both measures are reported.

These comparisons use the development folds, on which the strategy was
designed, and overlapping feature groups limit attribution to individual
inputs. Their transfer to later games is assessed through the later out-of-sample and
live evaluations; Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} discusses the broader limitations.

\subsection{Prediction Horizon}\label{sec:horizon-choice}

exposing the position to further game events. The trading comparison tests
this trade-off directly.

\section{Exchange Simulator}\label{sec:simulator}
The print-based fill rule cannot reconstruct queue position, the price paid
when taking liquidity, or the book available when an order reaches the
exchange. The event-driven simulator addresses these limitations by executing
the strategy against recorded book updates. It provides a more detailed
execution model, while retaining the limitation that other participants cannot
respond to the simulated orders.

\subsection{Design}
\paragraph{Timing.} Book updates follow exchange timestamps, so an accepted
order sees the recorded liquidity at its acceptance time. A 90 ms latency,
based on the sandbox's measured round-trip time, is assumed between a decision
and acceptance; slower settings provide a sensitivity check.

\paragraph{Fills.} A resting order joins the back of its price level. Recorded
trades consume the queue ahead; cancellations conservatively leave the assumed
queue position unchanged. Orders that cross the book consume available
liquidity and pay taker fees. The simulator also retains the through-print
fill condition. These rules distinguish obtaining a favourable quote from
actually receiving a fill at that price.

\paragraph{Fees.} Each fill uses the series-specific fee type and multiplier,
with the coefficients in Section~\ref{sec:execution} and the exchange's rounding
rules \citep{kalshi2026feeschedule}. Baseball spread and total contracts
pay taker fees only, at half the base multiplier; basketball and football
contracts pay both maker and taker fees. Accounting conventions are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:sim-implementation}.

\subsection{Validation and Limits}
Validation checks cover queue handling, fills and the fee ledger. On twelve
games, requiring fills to be backed by traded volume changed net returns from
6.05 to 6.00 cents per contract; 95\% of through-print fills were confirmed by
the recorded book shortly afterwards. Slower order latency also had little
effect on maker results in these checks. These findings support the replay
mechanics but do not validate market impact, exchange rejections or lost-message
behaviour, which are not modelled. Additional replay conventions are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:sim-implementation}.

\subsection{Comparison with Simple Market-Making Baselines}
A strategy that earns spread by quoting should be compared with quoting that has no information about the game.
For the baseline comparison, recorded games are rerun using three rules from
the market-making literature, with the same exchange simulator, fees and maker
entry and exit as the model. These rules use only the order book and trade
tape; nothing is trained or tuned.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Symmetric quote} \citep{avellaneda2008high} with no inventory or signal skew:
        a bid one cent below the last price and an offer one cent above, alternating on successive ticks.
  \item \textbf{Micro-price skew} \citep{stoikov2018micro}: quote on the side towards which the
        size-weighted mid of the best bid and ask leans, which reflects imbalance of the top of the book.
  \item \textbf{Trade-flow skew} \citep{cont2014price}: quote on the side of the net signed trade volume of the last 60 seconds.
\end{itemize}
The main comparison uses the model's decision times: each baseline trades on
the same contracts and at the same moments as the model, holding the timing
of opportunities fixed. Baselines place one contract per order, while the
model uses Section~\ref{sec:position-sizing}. Each strategy is evaluated on the
same recorded games. The comparison concerns fixed quoting rules; learned
market makers \citep{spooner2018market} fall outside its scope.

Trading returns provide a less uniform picture. Totals earn 3.69 cents per contract in
fold 1, fall to 1.18 and $-0.02$ cents in folds 2 and 3, and recover to 5.83 cents in fold 5

occasional large price moves can sustain trading returns even as average predictive
accuracy weakens.

Declining historical skill is consistent with a weakening forecasting edge, sometimes
described as \emph{alpha decay}, and weakens the case for treating full-period averages
as stable expectations. It does not by itself explain the forward loss, which is
well below even the weakest historical fold. The forward sample covers the following
NBA season. One possible explanation is that the distribution of total game scores in
previous seasons is not representative of the current season, reducing the
reliability of learned relationships for totals contracts. The forward sample also
uses received-time feeds and recorded-book execution, so changing game composition,
liquidity and execution can contribute. These results motivate monitoring skill and returns over time
without attributing the deterioration solely to faster information incorporation or
greater competition.

\section{Feed Availability}\label{sec:artefacts}
The live results expose variation in information arrival that aggregate
historical scores obscure. The responses described here were motivated by observed live
problems and require further forward evaluation.

Historical reconstruction assigns a fixed availability delay to each play.
Actual arrivals vary, and archived records can also differ from the provisional
records seen live. Baseball feeds include pending and revised calls, while
basketball observations often arrive in batches. A quiet feed can therefore
indicate either a pause in play or missing information, with different
implications for a trading decision.

The observed arrival delays motivate the 10-second MLB and 60-second NBA/NFL
settings described in Section~\ref{sec:data-latency}. Safeguards reduce exposure
to outdated state, but cannot recover information already used by faster
participants.

\section{Why Signals Shrink: Stale Prices and Adverse Selection}\label{sec:stale}
Even with timely game information, a forecast relative to the last trade need
not describe a move that remains available in the current book. In a diagnostic
rerun of one baseball game with fast inputs, the order-book midpoint was already
about 7 cents in the strategy's favour from the reference when an order was
placed, and then moved only about 1.5 cents over 120 seconds. Most of the
signal in this example was the distance between a stale last print and the
current market. The resting limit order one cent inside that reference sat behind
the market and filled selectively when the price reversed: filled orders lost 1.5 cents,
while unfilled signals were followed by favourable moves of 8 cents at 120
seconds, which are not attainable profits.
Taking at the touch instead lost 2.5 cents before fees and 5 after.

The second mechanism is adverse selection after a play. On the baseball test
folds, the trades that were held to settlement and lost were mostly entered just
after an in-play pitch: 49\% of the losing totals trades were filled within 15
seconds of one, against about 5\% of other trades, and most were short positions
in the over that were then decided against the strategy. Excluding fills within
15 seconds of an in-play pitch lifts simulated totals profit from 7.0 to
10.1 cents. This exclusion is a retrospective diagnostic, not an implementable
filter, since the runner does not yet know the play. The timing is consistent
with better-informed participants accepting stale quotes, although their
information is not observed.

These findings connect forecasting accuracy with execution: predictable changes
may already be in current quotes, and orders that fill can face different
outcomes from those that do not, so forecast skill, entry availability and
fill-conditional returns must be assessed together.


\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Summary of Findings}
This thesis examined whether in-game information and related contract prices
can predict short-horizon price changes in Kalshi sports markets, and whether
those forecasts can support trading after accounting for information delays,
fees and execution. The study covered MLB, NBA and NFL, combining a recurrent
representation of game history with message passing among winner, spread and
total contracts. Historical evaluation was complemented by live paper trading
and replay of recorded order books, allowing forecasting performance and
execution to be examined separately.

The historical results support predictability in spread and total prices
relative to a no-change forecast. Winner contracts show little comparable
skill at these horizons. Ablations indicate that the relationship between the
game state, contract thresholds and observed prices is particularly useful:
removing structural-reference inputs or contract message passing generally
reduces forecast skill and simulated returns. The contribution of play memory
is less consistent, while play-type labels add little once their effects on
the score, clock and win probability are represented. These findings support
modelling contracts within their shared game context, without establishing
that every architectural component is necessary or that the structural
reference represents a true fair value.

Under the historical fill and fee assumptions, the strategy earns positive
development returns across the six spread and total groups. Later out-of-sample
results are less uniform: baseball retains positive returns on both contract
types, football spreads are modestly positive but not clearly different from zero,
and football totals show no return. The
120-second horizon offers a useful compromise between forecast skill and
the size of price movements available after costs, rather than maximising
forecast skill alone. Returns also differ between the full forecast sample
and the signals selected for trading, which explains why improvements in
average prediction error do not always translate into higher profit.

The forward paper-trading evaluation, supported by recorded-order-book replay,
provides further evidence for the model's predictive signals. Net returns are
positive in four of the six league--contract groups: MLB spreads, NBA spreads,
and NFL spreads and totals (Table~\ref{tab:live}). MLB totals and NBA totals are
the exceptions, with negative net returns. The positive results across the
other groups support the model's predictive power on the signals selected
for trading, extending the historical evidence to later games under observed
feed arrivals and simulated book execution. This support is broad across the
three sports, although it is not uniform across contract types.

NBA's declining forecast skill across the historical folds also warns against
assuming a stable predictive edge. Its trading returns do not decline
uniformly, and the weaker forward results are concentrated in totals, so
this pattern is consistent with deterioration without identifying its cause.

The live observations and order-book replays also reveal limits to translating
predictive signals into executable returns.
A favourable forecast relative to the last trade may reflect a reference
price that is already stale, rather than a movement still available at an
executable quote. Resting orders can then remain unfilled during favourable
price moves and fill after subsequent information makes them attractive to
the other side. Delayed or revised game feeds compound this problem by
changing what the strategy knows when it places an order. Consequently,
positive historical returns do not by themselves establish reliable live
profitability. A central contribution of the study is the evaluation framework
that makes these differences visible, alongside the forecasting model itself.

\section{Limitations}
\paragraph{Development choices and statistical uncertainty.}
The walk-forward folds informed the architecture, input groups, trading rules
and sizing specification. Although each model was trained on earlier games,
these folds are development evidence for the selected system. Paired
comparisons reduce variation common to two specifications but do not remove
selection effects from examining many alternatives. Overlapping feature groups
also prevent a clean causal interpretation of individual ablations. Game-level
$t$-statistics and the scaling of risk measures rely on assumptions about
dependence between observations; shared trading conditions and games played
on the same day may weaken those assumptions. Isolated significant results
therefore require caution.

\paragraph{Evaluation coverage and protocol changes.}
The later out-of-sample period provides a separate check on transfer, but
includes MLB and NFL only. The NFL decision interval was revised after the
first evaluation, and the current quantity scale was adopted after the
original analysis. The baseball skip window was chosen on development data
and fixed before examining the later sample. Historical quantities are
recalculated on saved fill paths, so they do not capture every order that
would arise under the revised sizes. These qualifications limit the extent
to which the results constitute an untouched test of the final strategy.

\paragraph{Information availability and execution.}
The as-of protocol restricts forecasts to information available at the decision
time, but reconstructed historical availability cannot reproduce every delay,
revision or interruption encountered by live feeds. Historical fills are
inferred from trades, while the order-book simulator provides a closer
approximation using recorded liquidity and explicit queue assumptions.
Neither is a record of actual exchange execution. Historical returns depend
on the standardised fee and fill assumptions specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}. The simulator does not model market impact,
participant responses, exchange rejections or all recording failures. Keeping
order sizes below typical observed trade quantities reduces the scale of the
assumed intervention but does not validate these omissions.

\paragraph{Scope and external validity.}
The study concerns one venue and three sports, with trading restricted to
spreads and totals. The forward-test period is short, coverage depends on
usable recordings, and no real orders were submitted. Its results are useful
for identifying feed and execution mechanisms but cannot establish capacity,
stable profitability or performance in other seasons and market conditions.
Likewise, forecast skill against a last-trade benchmark is not direct evidence
of market inefficiency: the predictable movement may already be reflected in
quotes or may be unavailable after trading costs.

\section{Directions for Further Work}
Future work could bring the forecasting objective closer to the prices at
which orders can execute. Training targets could incorporate the current bid,
ask and midpoint alongside the last trade, while estimating fill probability
and returns conditional on a fill would help distinguish stale reference
prices from remaining trading opportunities. These estimates could also
inform order placement, cancellation and exits. Comparing forecasts with
order quantities and execution rules held fixed would help determine whether
model improvements translate into better trading outcomes independently of
sizing.

A longer prospective study would clarify whether the positive returns in
most contract groups persist beyond the present sample. Fixing the complete
specification before evaluation, retaining received-time feeds and order-book
records, and comparing models over the same opportunities would strengthen
this assessment. Further NBA observations would be particularly useful for
understanding the contrasting results for spreads and totals. More timely
game feeds would also allow a direct test of whether reducing information
delay improves executable returns. Uncertainty estimates that account for
dependence between games, together with risk measures based on a defined
capital allocation, would make these comparisons more informative.

Extending the study across seasons and liquidity conditions would help
establish when the learned relationships remain useful. For NBA totals,
comparing total-score distributions across seasons could test whether changes
in scoring patterns contribute to the forward losses. Such an analysis could
also examine whether retraining on more recent games improves transfer.
Comparisons with simpler models using the same structural inputs would
further clarify how much predictive value comes from the neural architecture
and how much comes from the information supplied to it.

\section{Historical Sizing Recalculation}\label{sec:historical-resizing}
Historical returns use the saved development and later out-of-sample fills.
Each stored fill receives its original signal-and-width
weight, multiplied by 20 and rounded half up to 0.01. Zero quantities are
excluded from profit and quantity aggregates. The saved entry and exit paths
are retained: opportunities previously blocked by an order that now rounds
to zero are not reconstructed. This fixed-fill calculation must therefore be
distinguished from a fresh chronological replay of the complete strategy.

Per-game profit includes eligible games without retained fills as zeros.
Net profit, profit per unit and paired statistics are
recomputed from the resized quantities. Historical fees retain the stated
unrounded per-unit assumptions; the series-specific fee and queue model belongs
to the separate evaluation on live-recorded order books.

The unsized comparison in Table~\ref{tab:sizing-evidence} instead simulates
orders without a signal cutoff and assigns one contract to each fill. Allowing
weak signals can occupy a contract before a stronger signal arrives, so these
fills differ from those of the selected strategy.

\section{Replay and Recording Details}\label{sec:sim-implementation}
Book reconstruction deduplicates exchange events before applying updates.
Limit prices follow the whole-cent grid, rounding buys down and sells up.
Simulated taker orders reduce the available displayed quantity so that
subsequent orders cannot reuse the same liquidity. The event ordering,
queue rules and fee ledger are checked separately from the forecasting model;
the empirical validation is reported in Section~\ref{sec:simulator}.

The fee ledger uses the exchange's per-order rounding and rebate accumulator,
with an assumed account balance precision of \$0.0001. The feed-guard comparison
retains each league's own checks: baseball waits for unresolved in-play pitches;
basketball and football suspend trading if any of the five latest plays arrived
more than 120 seconds late. Basketball also excludes review and placeholder
updates and requires agreement with the forecast based on real-time play stamps.

\section{Panel Construction Conventions}\label{sec:panel-conventions}
Trades sharing a timestamp are ordered by taker direction, price and trade
identifier to approximate sweep order. A contract-step row is retained when
it has a reference price and is either marked live or has a reference between
2 and 98 cents. The live indicator requires a two-sided, non-crossed quote
no wider than 10 cents, a midpoint between 3 and 97 cents, and quote age no
greater than five minutes. Missing or stale sides remain identifiable through
the model's quote-quality features.

five-seed forecast ensemble reduces squared error relative to no change.
For each valid target, let $d_j=y_j^2-(y_j-\widehat y_j)^2$, where $y_j$ is
the observed price change. Let $D_g$ and $n_g$ be the sum of these differences
and the number of observations in game $g$, and let $G$ be the game count.
The game-clustered paired statistic is
\begin{equation}
 t=\frac{\sum_g D_g}
 {\sqrt{\frac{G}{G-1}\sum_g(D_g-n_g\bar d)^2}},
 \qquad \bar d=\frac{\sum_g D_g}{\sum_g n_g}.
\end{equation}
Positive values favour the model. Clustering permits dependence among the
repeated times and contracts within a game; it assumes independence across
games and does not adjust for testing multiple horizons and contract types.
For MLB and NFL, the calculation uses the saved predictions and valid-target
masks from the later out-of-sample evaluation. For NBA, it pools the five disjoint
development test folds, using their saved ensemble forecasts and matching
panel targets. These NBA results remain development evidence.

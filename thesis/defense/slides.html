Machine Learning in Prediction Markets

Forecasting short-horizon price changes in Kalshi sports markets

Ng E En Casper

Advisor: Asst. Prof. Lu Yao · School of Computing, National University of Singapore

Good afternoon. My thesis asks a simple question about a new kind of market: can a model that watches a live sports game forecast where contract prices are heading in the next one to two minutes, and can that forecast actually be traded for a profit? I will show that the first is partly yes, and the second is much harder than it looks.

A prediction market contract pays one dollar if an event happens, so its price in cents reads as a probability. Sports now dominate these venues: Pew counts 80 percent of Kalshi volume and 39 percent of Polymarket volume. During a live game, every play changes the true probabilities, and prices have to follow. That reaction is what I study.

Research question

Can a forecast of price moves be traded?

1

Forecast

Does the model predict price changes over the next 30 to 120 seconds better than assuming no change?

2

Trade

Does that forecast survive fees, order fills and the delay with which live feeds report plays?

An accurate forecast need not be tradable, so the two are evaluated separately.

The study asks two questions in order. First, forecasting: does the model beat the simplest benchmark, which is that the price stays where it is? Second, trading: if it does, does that survive fees, whether our orders actually get filled, and the lag in live game data? I keep them separate on purpose, because the thesis shows that the first can hold while the second fails.

Existing work either predicts who wins a game and compares against bookmaker odds, or checks whether price levels are calibrated. Neither measures how well you can forecast a contract’s price change over the next minute or two, and neither asks whether the move is still available at a price you can actually trade. I contribute a model for this, an ablation study of where its skill comes from, and an evaluation that goes from historical backtests to live paper trading.

Forecast all three types, trade spreads and totals

For each game Kalshi lists a winner contract, plus ladders of spread contracts on the margin and total contracts on the combined score, so one game has many contracts that settle on the same result. I use Kalshi trades and one-minute bid and ask candles, with play-by-play from the MLB Stats API, ESPN and nflverse. The development sample is about 1,300 MLB games, 1,300 NBA games and 280 NFL games. The model forecasts all three contract types, but trading is restricted to spreads and totals, because winner contracts turned out to have almost no forecastable short-horizon move.

Forecasting task

Predict the price change over horizon h

Inputs: plays, game state, contract prices

Future trades build the label

horizon h

t decision time
reference price a(t)

t + h

t + h + 15 s

Target

Δ = volume-weighted price in window − a(t), in cents

Horizons

30, 60 and 120 s; trading uses 120 s

Skill

1 − (model error² ÷ “no change” error²)

At each decision time t, on a five-second grid, the model sees the plays, game state and contract prices available by t. The target is the change from the reference price at t to the volume-weighted traded price in a fifteen-second window that starts h seconds later, with h of 30, 60 or 120 seconds. Skill is the proportional reduction in squared error compared with predicting no change, so zero means no better than the last price. Trading uses the 120-second forecast.

A 10 s delay covers 97% of MLB arrivals (53% at 5 s). A model that used a play before the feed delivers it would show skill no live runner could reproduce.

The most important protocol rule is the as-of principle. A play can only be used once its result has happened and the feed would have delivered it. I measured arrival times on live feeds: MLB pitches arrive seven to fifteen seconds late, while ESPN basketball and football plays arrive after about 45 seconds, in batches. So each sport is trained and evaluated with a delay its live feed can actually meet: 10 seconds for MLB and 60 for NBA and NFL. Without this, the backtest would report skill that nobody could reproduce live.

I use three stages. The walk-forward folds train on earlier games and test on later ones, but because I used them to choose the model and the trading rule they count as development evidence only. The later out-of-sample period starts on 1 July 2026 and covers baseball and football; entry and exit rules were fixed before I looked at it, apart from one disclosed change to the NFL decision interval. Finally, live paper trading runs the strategy against real feed arrivals and recorded order books, with no real orders. All trading results are reported at the final sizing of 20 contracts at full weight.

Proposed method

Play memory plus cross-contract messages

Inputs

Plays so far, game state, contract prices and quotes, structural price reference

Play memory

A GRU summarises the plays into one game context shared by all contracts

Contract graph

Two rounds of message passing between winner, spread and total contracts

Two heads

Price change at 30, 60, 120 s; settlement probability as auxiliary task

One architecture, trained separately for MLB, NBA and NFL. Hidden size 64, five seeds averaged.

Here is the model at a glance. A recurrent network, a GRU, reads the plays that have arrived and compresses them into a game context. Every contract on the game gets its own representation built from its prices and quotes plus that shared context. Then the contracts exchange information through message passing, and two output heads read the result: one forecasts the price change at three horizons, and the other predicts the final settlement as an auxiliary task. A preliminary comparison favoured the GRU over a causal transformer. The same architecture is trained separately per sport.

Each contract receives all three averages, then updates itself. This repeats for two rounds.

Averaging works when the number of thresholds changes from game to game.

This is the relational part. Contracts on one game are the nodes of a graph. In each round, I average the representations within each contract type, so one summary for winners, one for spreads, one for totals. Every contract then receives all three summaries through learned matrices, and updates its own representation with a residual connection. I do this for two rounds. Averaging within types means the model copes with different ladder sizes per game, and the ablation later shows this is the most valuable architectural component.

Inputs and training

A structural price for every threshold

Given the score, time left and winner price, what should each threshold contract cost? The model sees this reference b and its gap from the last price a.

Spread contract, margin above k

b = 100 · Φ( s·z − k / (σ√τ) )

z from the winner price; τ = fraction of game remaining

Total contract, combined score above k

b = 100 · Φ( (S + Lτ − k) / (σ√τ) )

S = score so far; L = pregame total

Loss: penalise only prediction outside the 15 s trade range (Huber, 5¢)

Auxiliary: settlement log-loss, weight 0.1

Selection: early stopping on latest 15% of training games

One key input is a structural price reference. Using a simple normal approximation of scoring, I convert the current score, time remaining and winner price into an approximate probability for each spread or total threshold, on the same scale as the market price. The model is given this reference and its gap from the last traded price, and is free to learn departures from it. For training, I use an interval loss that only penalises forecasts falling outside the range of trades in the target window, with a Huber penalty, plus a small settlement loss. Early stopping uses the latest fifteen percent of the training games.

Skill transfers unevenly. Baseball keeps most of its skill on the later out-of-sample period: spreads go from 7.5 to 6.5 percent and totals from 6.7 to 4.8. Football deteriorates sharply: spread skill drops from 6.3 to 1.5, and totals go to about zero, though that sample is just 64 games. Basketball has no later sample, but within the development folds its skill falls in every successive fold, from 16 to 3 percent for spreads. So the full-period averages overstate what one should expect going forward.

Historical results: trading

From forecast to simulated trade

1 · Signal

Every 5 s: forecast price minus last trade. Act if at least 5¢.

2 · Enter

Resting limit order 1¢ inside the reference price, for up to 60 s.

3 · Exit

Resting exit 120 s after entry; fall back to taking liquidity or settlement.

4 · Size

20 × weight contracts. Weight grows with signal, from 5¢ to 15¢.

Why resting orders: at 50¢ a taker pays 1.75¢ per contract, a maker 0.44¢

MLB skip window: no orders 15 to 60 s after the last known pitch

20 contracts is below the median trade size (25 to 49)

Positive skill does not establish tradability, so I define a simple rule. Every five seconds I compare the 120-second forecast with the last traded price, and act when the gap is at least five cents. Entry is a resting limit order one cent inside the reference price, which matters because fees are roughly four times lower for makers at 50 cents. Exit is another resting order 120 seconds after entry, with fallbacks. Size is 20 contracts times a weight that grows with signal strength, kept below the median observed trade size. In baseball I also skip decisions when a pitch has probably been thrown but my feed has not yet shown it, since the market has already priced it.

I retrain the model with one piece removed and compare. The biggest losses come from removing the structural reference group, which cuts skill by 2 to 7 points in all six groups; for football, skill falls from about 8 percent to about 1. Removing message passing costs 1.4 to 4.2 points in every group and significantly lowers profit in three. Play memory helps baseball but not consistently elsewhere, and play-type labels add almost nothing once score, clock and win probability are included. One caveat: the feature groups overlap, so I cannot cleanly attribute to individual inputs.

Two sensitivity checks. For feed delay, each delay is a separately trained model. For basketball and football, profit at 45 seconds is close to the 60-second operating models, so the edge does not rely on the very latest play. Baseball is more sensitive: skill falls steadily as delay grows, and the live feed is seven to fifteen seconds late. For horizon, the 120-second strategy earns more per contract than the 30-second one in all six groups, even though forecast skill is lower, because larger moves cover costs. This does not prove longer holding is the cause.

Order-book simulator

Reported returns come from the simulator, run on recorded live feeds.

Simulator

  • Queue position at each price
  • 90 ms order latency
  • Taker fee when crossing the book

Not modelled: market impact

Historical fill rules cannot say where an order sits in the queue or what price a taker would really pay. So I recorded live game feeds and Kalshi order books in the order they arrived, ran the forecasting model live as paper trading, and then replayed the recorded books through an event-driven exchange simulator. It models queue position, a 90 millisecond latency, taker fees when an order crosses the book, and series-specific fees. In twelve validation games, 95 percent of through-print fills were confirmed by the recorded book. It does not model market impact: other participants cannot react to our orders.

Over three weeks of live recordings, I evaluated 10 baseball games, 107 basketball games and 42 football games. Net returns are positive in four of six groups: MLB spreads, NBA spreads, and both NFL contract types. MLB totals and NBA totals lose money, and NBA totals lose the most at about 7.5 cents per contract. Baseball was already in the postseason, leaving only ten games, so those results are descriptive. For NBA totals, the historical skill decay is consistent with a weakening edge but does not by itself explain a loss this large.

A strategy that earns by quoting should be compared with quoting that has no information. I reran the recorded games with three fixed rules from the market-making literature, using the same simulator, fees, games and decision times. Every baseline loses after fees. Dropping those fills lifts simulated totals from 7.0¢ to 10.1¢, but that is a hindsight diagnostic, not a usable filter.

Two mechanisms explain why live returns are smaller than the historical forecast skill suggests. First, the reference price is the last trade, which can be stale. In a diagnostic baseball game, the order-book midpoint had already moved about seven cents in my favour when I placed the order, and only about one and a half cents of movement remained over the next two minutes. My resting order sat behind the market and filled when the price reversed, so filled orders lost money while unfilled signals looked good. Second, adverse selection: nearly half of the losing baseball totals trades were filled within fifteen seconds after a pitch, versus about five percent of other trades, consistent with better-informed participants hitting stale quotes. The lesson is that forecast skill, entry availability and fill-conditional returns must be judged together.

Conclusion

Skill is real, but it is not profit

1

Predictable: spread and total prices beat “no change”; winner prices do not.

2

Why: the structural price reference and message passing drive the skill.

3

Returns: positive in development; less uniform later; four of six groups live.

4

Limits: stale prices, feed delay and adverse selection cap executable returns.

To summarise. First, spread and total prices are predictable over 30 to 120 seconds relative to no change; winner prices are not. Second, the structural reference and cross-contract message passing are what the skill depends on, while play-type labels add little. Third, historical simulated returns are positive, but later out-of-sample results are less uniform, and live paper trading is positive in four of six groups, losing on MLB and NBA totals. Fourth, and most important, accuracy does not equal tradability: stale reference prices, delayed feeds and adverse selection limit what can be captured. The evaluation framework that makes these gaps visible is as much a contribution as the model.

Limitations and future work

What this study cannot yet claim

Limitations

  • Development folds informed the model and rules
  • Forward sample is short: three weeks, and only 10 MLB games
  • Replay cannot model market impact
  • NBA skill decays; the cause is unknown

Future work

  • Train on bid, ask and midpoint, not only the last trade
  • Model fill probability and fill-conditional returns
  • Longer prospective test with the specification fixed in advance
  • Faster feeds, to test whether less delay raises executable returns
The main limitations: the walk-forward folds shaped the design, so they are development evidence; the forward sample is only three weeks and baseball was already in the postseason; the simulator cannot capture market impact; and NBA skill decays without a clear cause. For future work, the most natural step is to bring the training target closer to executable prices, using bid, ask and midpoint, and to model fill probability so that stale signals can be told apart from real opportunities. A longer prospective study with a specification fixed in advance would settle whether the positive live groups persist.
